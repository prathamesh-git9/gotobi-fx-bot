# Swap-free trend research (checked 21 October 2026)

## Exness terms

Exness says swap-free status applies across a Personal Area and is the default for all accounts. Its current eligible list includes **all seven FX majors** (AUDUSD, EURUSD, GBPUSD, NZDUSD, USDCAD, USDCHF, USDJPY), gold (XAUUSD), silver (XAGUSD), and **all indices**. Thus the proposed broader universe is also currently eligible; calling it a non-eligible universe would be incorrect. The separate Islamic-account rules differ. Status depends on trading activity: Exness recommends trading mostly during the day and minimizing overnight orders; regularly holding large amounts overnight can disqualify an account. It says an algorithm reviews activity, notifies the client in the Personal Area, and cancellation makes swaps applicable to *current and future* positions. The public page gives **no numerical definition** of “large,” “regularly,” or a holding-day limit, so a weekly trend book held for months cannot safely assume enduring zero financing. [Exness swap-free status](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/4402341895570-Swap-free-status).

Exness publishes Standard-account swap rates in pips, but says they can change daily. The publicly indexed table displayed EURUSD **-0.58/0**, GBPUSD **-0.21/-0.06**, USDJPY **0/0** pips per night (long/short). Its commodity table displayed XAUUSD **-55.04/0** pips (gold pip = $0.01/oz). These are snapshots, **not historical swap series**. On live rendering the rows may require client-side loading, and I could not reliably retrieve current numeric US500 and USTEC long/short entries; those rates are therefore **unverified**, not zero. A 0 shown in a table also does not guarantee that every account and date will receive free financing. [Exness forex table](https://www.exness.com/forex/); [Exness commodity table](https://www.exness.pro/commodities/); [Exness index table](https://www.exness.com/indices/); [Exness swap explanation](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/360014709151-About-swap).

Standard accounts list a general 0.01-lot minimum, but instrument-specific index minima override that: US500 **0.14**, USTEC **0.05**, DE30 **0.07**, UK100 **0.05** lot. Index contract size is 1 index unit per lot. Standard FX 0.01 lot is 1,000 base-currency units; XAUUSD 0.01 lot is 1 troy ounce. Standard Cent's 0.01 **cent lot** is 1/100 of a Standard 0.01 lot by the published 1-cent-lot = 0.01 standard-lot relationship, enabling roughly 10 base-currency units of FX and 0.01 oz gold. Exness's Cent page lists FX, gold and silver; its newer index-specific page additionally says US500 and USTEC are available on Cent **only via MT5 and Exness platforms**, though that page also retains a contradictory generic sentence saying indices are not offered on Cent. Confirm Cent contract sizing, minimums, and instrument availability in the actual terminal before trading. DE30 and UK100 are absent from Cent. [Standard](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/17537782738460-Standard-account); [Standard Cent](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/17537782786588-Standard-Cent-account); [indices](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/17854383867548-Indices); [gold](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/17854173039388-Commodities); [contract sizes](https://get.exness.help/hc/en-us/articles/360014740179-Common-trading-terms).

At about $1,000 EUR capital, a Standard minimum FX ticket consumes roughly an entire account's notional; 1 oz gold costs several account values; an index minimum is hundreds to thousands of dollars depending on level. Margin leverage permits opening them but does not make their **risk granularity** small enough for an eight-market 12%-volatility book. Cent can make FX and gold granular; US500/USTEC need terminal confirmation. No Cent access to DE30/UK100 means the complete eight-market strategy still cannot be copied there.

## Evidence and expectations

[Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012, *Journal of Financial Economics*)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.11.003) documented 1–12 month continuation in 58 liquid futures/forwards over 1985–2009. Their diversified 12-month strategy had an annual **gross Sharpe above 1**, with large gains in 2008's later collapse and sharp losses at the 2009 reversal. This is broad institutional futures evidence, not a forecast for a small CFD subset; their paper's earlier 1966–85 extension used fewer markets and reported Sharpe 1.1. [Full paper](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/mosk.pdf).

[Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017, *Journal of Portfolio Management*)](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2017.44.1.015) reconstructed multi-asset trend portfolios from 1880 through 2016, reporting positive average returns in every decade and gains in eight of the ten largest historical 60/40 crises. The average *single-market* gross Sharpe was about **0.4**, while even the diversified strategy experienced drawdowns reaching about **25%**. Its many futures include bonds and commodities missing here, so its long-run risk/return and crisis protection cannot be transferred directly to this CFD portfolio. [Author-hosted paper](https://images.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/Journal-Article/AQR-JPM-Fall-2017.pdf).

Post-2015 evidence is mixed: [Babu et al. (2020), *You Can't Always Trend When You Want*](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/You-Cant-Always-Trend-When-You-Want) attribute weak 2010s trend returns to fewer sustained large market moves, rather than claiming the effect vanished. [AQR's updated monthly TSMOM factor dataset](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Datasets/Time-Series-Momentum-Factors-Monthly) extends beyond the original paper, but is a diversified academic series rather than an Exness implementation. A small retail subset should conservatively expect **Sharpe around 0–0.5 after costs, possibly negative**, and **drawdowns of tens of percent** at leveraged 12% target risk; these are cautious planning ranges, not claims from either paper.
